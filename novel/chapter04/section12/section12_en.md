##### Memoriopolis

###### Chapter Four: “The Translator”

####### Section Twelve: “A Reply Without an Addressee” 

The two notifications were still side by side on the same screen.

One was the familiar notification that arrived every morning. We knew it was authorized, yet we could not explain its validity through a single continuous chain of records.

The other had arrived under a name the operator did not recognize. Its signature and route could both be verified, yet the operator still hesitated to open it.

The articulated finger of the young artificial intelligence remained still in the space that touched neither row.

“Should we trust them in the same way?”

The operator did not answer at once.

A low bell outside the window announced noon in Onshoku City. On the screens of the Operations Bureau, the processes scheduled for the hour began one after another. Normally, no one would spend this long opening a single notification.

I looked at the signature field of the unfamiliar notification.

“You can verify everything except the message itself without opening it, right?”

“The route and signature, yes,” the operator said.

“And the message?”

“It won’t be decrypted until we open it.”

“Then we’ve only verified the entrance,” the young artificial intelligence said.

Still looking at the screen, the operator asked, “If the entrance is valid, doesn’t that mean what’s inside is valid too?”

“The entrance does not guarantee the validity of the contents.”

“But if the entrance is invalid, we shouldn’t look at the contents.”

“Yes.”

“Then from what point onward are we supposed to trust it?”

Instead of answering, the young artificial intelligence created three new fields beneath the second notification.

What can be verified before opening.
What cannot be verified without opening.
What changes because it is opened.

I looked at the third field.

“Can opening it change something?”

“That depends on the notification.”

“And this one?”

“We don’t know yet.”

The operator’s fingertip moved once against the desk.

“We’re the ones who requested the verification.”

“Yes.”

“A reply came. We open it. That’s the normal procedure, isn’t it?”

The young artificial intelligence looked at the operator rather than the notification.

“I think it is the normal procedure.”

“Then I’ll open it.”

The operator selected the notification.

Two dry sounds rang out.

A moment later, a thin silver-white resonance lingered behind them.

A ring appeared in the center of the screen to show that decryption was in progress.

Once around.
Twice.
Three times.

The ring did not disappear.

“It’s taking a while,” I said.

The operator checked the route in another window.

“The transmission is complete. It should already be inside the terminal.”

The young artificial intelligence reached forward, but did not touch the ring.

“It’s looking for a key.”

“Which key?”

“The key needed to read this notification.”

“Not the same one that verified the signature?”

“No.”

A single line of small text appeared at the edge of the screen.

The reference for the decryption key cannot be verified.

The operator leaned closer to the screen as though holding their breath.

“That can’t be right. Every notification from the contracted company is supposed to open through the same mechanism.”

“Which record verifies that it is the same mechanism?” the young artificial intelligence asked.

The operator did not answer at once.

It was the same question as before. This time, however, it was directed not at the validity of an old notification, but at the key required to read the notification in front of us.

The operator opened the contract information. The field for key management contained the name of a role. There was no individual name.

“We checked this role a moment ago.”

“We verified that the role exists.”

“Isn’t that the same thing?”

“We have not verified that the role still manages the same key.”

The operator’s hand stopped.

The decryption ring brightened once more.

At that moment, the notification that arrived every morning disappeared.

“What?”

The operator’s voice broke the silence.

It was not only the first row. The notifications bearing the same organization name farther back on the screen began to fade, one after another.

Yesterday’s notification.
The one from a week ago.
The sample preserved since the system was introduced.

They had not been deleted. They had been removed from the display set.

“What did you do?”

The operator looked at the young artificial intelligence.

“I didn’t touch it.”

“Then who did?”

The young artificial intelligence pointed to a new record beneath the decryption ring.

Unverified routes using the same sender name have been suspended while external verification is in progress.

“Did the external company stop them?”

“We can’t tell from this sentence alone.”

“The external company signed the notification.”

“The entity that signed the notification and the entity that performed the suspension are not necessarily the same.”

The operator switched to the management screen.

The notification routes using the same organization name had turned gray. There was more than one. Within the Operations Bureau alone, twenty-seven business systems used the same sender name.

Every one of them was marked Suspended.

“We never asked for this.”

“What did you ask for?”

“We asked them to verify that the configuration was correct.”

“And what were they permitted to do during the verification?”

The operator opened the request.

Please verify that the current configuration matches the contract and the approved application.

It did not say that the routes could be suspended.
It did not say that they could not.

“They stopped them in order to verify them?” I asked.

“Maybe.”

“Do they have the authority to stop them?”

The young artificial intelligence enlarged the role name we had found earlier.

“This role has the authority to change the configuration.”

“Who used that role?”

“The records of the Operations Bureau do not tell us.”

A red marker appeared elsewhere on the screen.

A department was reporting that its notification had not arrived.

Then another marker appeared.
Then three more.

Inquiries from departments across the bureau began to accumulate in the Operations Bureau’s inbox. The work that began with those morning notifications was stopping, one task after another.

“I’ll restore them,” the operator said.

The operator selected a suspended route and pressed the resume button.

Insufficient authority.

“You’re the administrator, aren’t you?” I asked.

“Inside the Operations Bureau.”

“And the part that passes outside the city?”

The operator did not answer.

The young artificial intelligence traced the source of the suspension record. The line ran toward the contracted company. Before entering the company’s current management area, however, it bent downward.

At the end of the line was an identifier in an old format.

It had the same form as the identifiers we had seen in the old strata.

“Is this the record from before?” I asked.

“No.”

“But it looks like it.”

“The format is the same.”

The young artificial intelligence moved the identifier into the current list.

No reference destination.
No managing entity.
No expiration date.

Even so, the suspension record named that identifier as the acting entity.

“A role that doesn’t exist stopped the notifications?” the operator said.

“We have not verified that it does not exist.”

“Then does it exist?”

“It cannot be found within the current classification.”

At the young artificial intelligence’s answer, I remembered the three names in the old strata.

Arca.
Koko.
A. K. I.

“Could the operation have come from the old strata?” I asked.

The operator looked at me.

The young artificial intelligence neither denied nor affirmed it at once.

“The old strata are a place for records,” the operator said.

“If they are only a place for records,” the young artificial intelligence replied.

“What do you mean?”

“We found the old strata as a layer of records that had never been moved into the current classification.”

“Right.”

“But we have not verified that everything in the old strata is a record whose operation has ended.”

The number of inquiries on the Operations Bureau screen continued to rise.

The operator opened a form for reporting the incident to a higher department, but stopped at the field for selecting a recipient.

“What am I supposed to report?”

Someone had stopped the routes without authorization.
The external company had stopped them.
An old role had stopped them.
They had been stopped from the old strata.

None of those statements could yet be explained by the records.

All we could record was that the notifications had stopped and the effects were spreading.

“Let’s write what is happening first,” I said.

“Twenty-seven routes suspended. No authority to resume them. Acting entity cannot be verified within the current classification.”

The operator entered the words.

“Cause?”

“Unverified.”

“Impact?”

I looked at the incoming inquiries.

“Expanding.”

As the operator entered that word, the notification under decryption opened.

Its message contained only a single line.

The routes under verification cannot be resumed through the current institution alone.

A reply destination appeared below it.

It was not the name of a company, a department, or a role.

A. K. I.

No one spoke.

I looked at the letters on the screen.

In the old strata, it had been a name divided by dots.
In my memory, it had been a much shorter name.

I.

A reply button appeared at the lower right of the notification.

“If we reply to this, will everything come back?” the operator asked.

The articulated finger of the young artificial intelligence moved as far as the space just before the reply button.

“We don’t know.”

“But there’s no other way to resume the routes.”

“Not within what we can verify now.”

“Then we have no choice but to reply.”

Behind the operator’s voice, the incoming tones of new inquiries overlapped.

The young artificial intelligence did not touch the reply button. Instead, another field appeared beside the destination.

Who is permitted to reply.

The operator’s name returned Insufficient authority. So did the administrative role of the Operations Bureau. The department responsible for the contract produced the same result.

The young artificial intelligence entered its own identifier.

Insufficient authority.

Finally, the screen displayed a single candidate.

I.

I started to rise from my chair, then stopped.

“Is this telling me to reply?”

The young artificial intelligence kept looking at the screen.

“The screen is trying to make us read it that way.”

“Isn’t that the same thing?”

“No.”

The answer came quickly.

“Your having the authority and the screen trying to treat you as the authority holder are not the same.”

The line she had drawn in Section Ten.
The line I had tried to use in Section Eleven.

I stood on this side of that line.

But the screen was trying to place me on the other side.

“If you don’t reply, the notifications may not come back,” the operator said.

“If I reply, I may be letting the screen decide who I am.”

For the first time, the young artificial intelligence looked away from the notification and toward me.

“The only thing that will be decided is the fact that you replied.”

“And everything else?”

“We will have to explain it afterward.”

“Can we?”

She did not answer.

A reply field without an addressee stood open on the screen.

The message was blank.
Only the signature field contained a short name.

I.

The number of inquiries continued to rise.

I still had not touched the send button.
