## E0002 Another Track

On my commute, I have been reading a little at a time from 『午餐的敦子』, *Greek Lessons*, and 『新しい階級社会』.

That day, I changed from the Chiyoda Line to the Yurakucho Line. At Ginza-itchome Station, two passengers boarded together. They took hold of the straps and began talking directly in front of me.

They were loud.

Before long, my ears began to hurt.

The conversation went roughly like this:

“I think marriage is really just about whether someone will be there beside you when you die.”

“I know exactly what you mean.”

“Money is there to be spent. Let's go to an *okama* bar next time.”[^okama]

“The people who work at places like that are smart, aren't they?”

Of course, I cannot infer the shape of their lives or the values they hold from a brief conversation overheard on a train. I knew nothing about what kind of work they did, what sort of families they came from, or what they had lived through.

Even so, their conversation left me with a question.

What is marriage?

Having someone beside you when you die matters. But having raised a son, I cannot think about marriage and family only in terms of life's final moments.

Biological reproduction is one of the functions that marriage as an institution has historically served. Yet for human beings, bringing a child into the world is not enough to make that child a member of the next society.

It takes about twenty years to raise one human being.

Parents are better able to do that if they remain alive. A home is needed. Someone has to work to sustain daily life. At the same time, someone also has to spend time with the child.

Human beings are mammals whose young require a long period of care. Human society is also extraordinarily complex. Children need years to learn how to use language, negotiate distance from other people, follow rules, take on roles, avoid danger, handle tools, and live within institutions.

Installing the social operating system takes a long time.

Perhaps the generation before mine was more likely to assume that if money was earned and brought home, the next generation would somehow grow up on its own. That was certainly not true of everyone. Still, for a long time, society treated one person working long hours for a company and another person raising children at home as its standard model.

Greater equality between women and men changed that model profoundly.

More people gained access to education, the freedom to choose a profession, the ability to earn their own income, and the right to speak openly in public. Even being able to discuss marriage, money, and leisure without restraint on a train may be one expression of that expanded freedom.

But this raises another question.

What happens if more people are allowed to enter the competition, while the playing field itself still assumes a person who does not carry responsibility for care?

The worker imagined by a company is always available. The ideal worker can respond to sudden demands, work long hours, relocate when necessary, and keep everything outside work from entering the workplace.

A person caring for a baby or a young child cannot live that way.

Children do not sleep according to company schedules. They get sick. Sometimes they need someone to listen. There are meals, baths, medical appointments, trips to and from nursery school, play, study, arguments, and reconciliation.

It is difficult to divide work and childcare evenly at all times.

During the most demanding years, childcare may take ninety percent of a person's capacity, or all of it. A more realistic system would allow people to hand their work over to trusted colleagues for a time and return when the burden of care has eased.

What we need is not the celebration of superhuman workers who manage everything alone.

We need systems that make it possible to hand work over safely, sustain a household during the years of intensive childcare, protect a person's position and path back into the workplace, avoid concentrating the entire burden on the colleagues who take over, and refuse to treat the experience of raising a child as a blank space in a career.

The phrase “women's advancement” has often meant enabling women to enter an existing model of success. But if that model was built around people who could leave care work at home to someone else, increasing the number of people allowed to participate is not enough.

There are more pieces to choose from now.

But the rules of the board may still be old.

There was something else in the conversation that stayed with me.

People work, earn money, spend it, and seek relief from the tensions of daily life in spaces built around hospitality and performance. In some of those spaces, people perform ways of speaking and gendered roles that differ from those of ordinary life.

It seemed to me that several layers of performance were overlapping.

At work, people adapt themselves to an older, male-centered model of success. Outside work, they loosen the resulting tension in another space of performance. Customers and performers alike make use of the roles society has handed them, sometimes reversing those roles in order to create the space between them.

But I have never been to the place they were discussing, and I do not know what the two passengers themselves were looking for. What I saw was not their inner lives. It was a structure that took shape in my own mind, prompted by a brief conversation.

Marriage.

Childcare.

Work.

Money.

Consumption.

Gender roles.

The forms of success offered by society.

They connected into a single, twisted line.

I know nothing about the lives of those two people. It would be presumptuous of me to evaluate them.

Still, as I listened, I felt that we had not yet built an adequate bridge between a society in which everyone is increasingly able to work in the same way and a society that can continue only if someone raises the next generation.

The train was running on the Yurakucho Line.

But what I was looking at was another track, laid between competition and care.

[^okama]: *Okama bar* is a Japanese nightlife term without a precise English equivalent. Here it refers broadly to a venue where gender-nonconforming performance and conversation may form part of the entertainment. The word *okama* can be derogatory when applied to a person, although some performers and venues use it as a self-description.

### Production Notes

#### Starting Point of the Observation

A conversation about marriage, money, and leisure overheard between two passengers on the Yurakucho Line after Ginza-itchome Station.

#### Distinguishing Observation, Memory, and Inference

- The route, the passengers' position in the carriage, and the loud conversation are based on the author's observation and memory.
- The dialogue was not recorded. It has been reconstructed from memory to preserve its substance rather than its exact wording.
- The passengers' occupations, family situations, values, gender identities, and life histories were not verified.
- The statement that raising a child takes about twenty years is a broad formulation based on the author's experience of raising a child and on the long period of human development and socialization.
- The discussion of generations, models of work, care, social participation, and spaces of consumption consists of the author's reflections and hypotheses arising from the conversation. It is not intended as a definitive account of the two passengers.

#### Provisional Central Claim

Even if women and men are able to compete on the same playing field, society has not changed enough if that field still assumes competitors who bear no responsibility for care.

#### Provisional Ideas for Institutional Design

- During periods of intensive childcare, do not demand a nominal fifty-fifty balance between work and care.
- Establish systems for safe handover and support the colleagues who take over the work.
- Provide material security during periods of childcare.
- Protect a person's employment status, conditions, and professional path upon returning.
- Do not treat care experience as an empty period or a loss of ability in a career.
- Make these systems available to anyone who assumes responsibility for raising a child, regardless of gender.
