# E0001 From “Romaru” to the Roma

It was either yesterday or the day before.

On the Yurakucho Line on my way home, I could hear a conversation between two people sitting beside me. They were speaking so loudly that my right ear was beginning to feel as though it could not take much more.

They were talking about how to attract younger colleagues at work.

At several points, they also mentioned wanting to get married soon. From what I could gather, neither of them was currently seeing anyone.

I was about to move to another seat when an unfamiliar word caught my ear.

“I could totally *romaru* with someone ten years younger. I’m confident.”

*Romaru.*

At least, that was what it sounded like to me.

Was it a newly coined verb derived from “romance”? I searched for it, but I could not find a meaning that fit the conversation.

Instead, the search results led me to the Roma.

When I travelled through Europe as a student, I once saw Roma asking passengers for money on a train.

Ever since then, that scene and the word “Roma” have remained linked in my memory.

Looking into the subject again, I found that linguistic and historical research generally traces the origins of the Roma to northern India, followed by a long westward migration.

Reference:
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people#Origin

When I opened the English-language article, it appeared to contain far more information than the Japanese version.

I dislike seeing everything absorbed into the framework of the nation-state.

At the same time, thinking about the Roma led me to consider the difference between communities rooted in a particular place and communities that move across places.

In an age when agriculture and material resources were tightly bound to the land, communities able to occupy and maintain the same territory over time must have had easier access to the foundations of everyday life.

People who moved from place to place may have had to survive outside those land-based systems of production by combining other skills, including trade, craft, performance, labour, and services.

This is not meant as an account of the occupations or ways of life of Roma people as a whole. It is only a provisional hypothesis that grew out of one scene I witnessed on a train many years ago.

Today, agriculture is increasingly mechanised, and people from settled communities also work in services, information, finance, technology, and many other fields that are not directly tied to the soil.

With English and access to digital communication, a person can leave the place of birth and work across several countries and institutional systems.

If mobility is considered in spatial terms alone, even people who appear settled have become more mobile than before.

Yet that mobility does not amount to complete freedom.

People who make a living through social media may appear detached from any particular territory. At the same time, their activities remain bound to followers, view counts, platform rules, and recommendation algorithms.

They may have left one kind of ground only to become attached to another.

That new ground is not soil. It is made of numbers, rules, and audiences.

Seen from a slight distance, an influencer may appear less like an entirely free-moving person and more like someone whose livelihood depends on receiving a continuous supply of attention from other people.

This does not mean that such a person is the same as someone asking for money on a train.

The two should not be conflated.

Still, there may be a distant structural resemblance in the need to keep receiving something from others in order to sustain one’s life.

To be free of the land is not to be free of all constraint.

Leave the nation, and one becomes connected to the market.
Leave the market, and one becomes connected to a community.
Leave the community, and one becomes connected to a platform.

People do not lose the ground beneath them.
They change the ground to which they are connected.

I heard a word whose meaning I did not know: *romaru*.

I still do not know what it meant.

But while searching for it, I remembered the Roma, began thinking about land and movement, and eventually arrived at social-media followers.

I never found the meaning of the word.

Even so, the word opened a path.
