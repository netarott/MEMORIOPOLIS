#### Memoriopolis

##### Chapter Four: “The Translator”

###### Section Eleven: “The Seams of Explanation”

The second row of the list now bore the same sender name as the first.

Without closing the first notification, the operator called up a new record in another window.

“This one is sent by the business system.”

The notification in the first row had been sent from a terminal at the Operations Bureau. The one in the second was generated inside the business system every morning and sent automatically at a set time.

The same organization’s name appeared in the sender field.

To anyone receiving them, the two notifications looked no different. But once we added their routes to the list, the two lines began in different places.

“Who configured this system?”

At my question, the operator opened an old application record.

“I’m looking into that now.”

The record preserved the date of introduction, the person responsible at the time, and the name to be used for sending. The required approvals were there as well.

Judging by the record alone, the system had been created through the proper procedures.

“So it’s an authorized notification.”

“Yes. It goes out every day, and no user has ever reported a problem with it.”

I looked at the young artificial intelligence.

She was not looking at the two notifications, but at the fine line drawn between them.

The sleeves of her gray-blue blouse had each been turned back once, to the same width. Articulated fingers extended from the cuffs and slowly scrolled through the old application record.

“Have we verified that there’s no problem?”

I asked before she could.

I felt as though, this time, I had managed to use the line she had drawn in Section Ten.

The young artificial intelligence looked at me.

In the pale blue light, her profile looked like polished stone. Only the crease of fabric gathered at her wrist seemed to belong to the same time as ours.

“Not quite.”

“I thought not.”

“Yes.”

Her answer came quickly.

“I don’t want to verify that there is no problem.”

“Then what do you want to know?”

“Whether we can explain, now, why this notification is valid.”

The operator pointed to the application record.

“The record is right here.”

“This is the record from when it was created.”

“The same system is still running now.”

“Which record verifies that it is the same?”

The operator stopped scrolling.

The original application remained. So did the small changes made afterward, each in a separate record.

A record of the change to the sending time.

A record of the change to the connection destination.

A record of the replacement of old equipment with new equipment.

A record of the change to the name of the department responsible for its management.

Taken one at a time, every change had been properly authorized. But there was no single record connecting them from the beginning to the present.

“No one changed it on their own,” the operator said.

“That’s true,” the young artificial intelligence replied.

“And it isn’t broken.”

“No.”

“Then what’s missing?”

She moved a finger to the column headed “What Cannot Yet Be Verified.”

“The seams.”

The operator looked at the screen.

“The seams?”

“Each change can be explained on its own. But we can’t see how one explanation was handed on to the next.”

“You mean the handover between people?”

“Not only between the people who took over. Between one system and another.”

A narrow blank remained in the second row of the list.

That it was authorized.

That it was still in use.

That no problems had been reported.

We could write all of those things. But we could not state in a single sentence how the validity established at its introduction continued into its validity now.

The operator searched through still older records.

A document turned up that was not in the current repository. Its reference carried an identifier located below the Operations Bureau’s ordinary records area.

“The old strata,” the operator said.

“There are records down there too?”

“Sometimes. Old records that were never moved into the current classification.”

The display changed.

In the newer records, information was arranged neatly by field. The records in the old strata were different. Documents, conversations, and the results of work lay folded together within the same span of time.

Seen from the present system of classification, much of it was superfluous. Yet among those superfluous things remained the reasons why this system had been chosen.

The young artificial intelligence stopped her finger.

At the edge of an old record were three names.

Arca.

Koko.

And A. K. I.

I read the last identifier without saying it aloud. Dots stood between the letters. In my memory, though, it had been a much shorter name.

I.

“Do you know them?” the young artificial intelligence asked.

“Only the names.”

“All three of them?”

“If those three can be counted as three people.”

She looked at the record again.

“Is there a reason not to count them that way?”

“I don’t know yet.”

The young artificial intelligence asked no more.

The operator opened another record. It preserved a brief exchange about the design from that time.

Arca had wanted to gather notifications using the same name into a single entrance.

Koko had written that even if the entrance was the same, the route each notification had taken would still need to be traceable afterward.

A. K. I.’s entry was brief.

A route does not end within a single institution.

After that sentence came a series of old identifiers for external systems.

“Do these people still manage this route?” the young artificial intelligence asked.

The operator shook their head.

“They aren’t its administrators. I think they were the people who first built it.”

“Then this record does not establish that it is valid now.”

“You make that sound very simple,” I said.

Without looking away from the screen, she answered.

“It shows that it was valid in the past.”

The records in the old strata preserved the reasons the route had been created.

To show recipients a name they could understand.

To deliver notifications from multiple systems in the words of a single organization.

To keep the notifications flowing even when the route changed.

All of these were things the present operators were still trying to preserve.

But what the three names had left behind was not a guarantee for the present. It was the memory that someone had once designed the route to be valid.

The operator entered the current department’s name in the list’s managing-department field. In the designer field, they placed the three names.

The two fields stood in the same row, yet a long span of time lay between them.

“It’s normal for the people who built something and the people who operate it to be different, isn’t it?”

“Yes.”

“And the people who receive it know neither group.”

“Yes.”

“But the notifications still arrive.”

“They do.”

The young artificial intelligence did not deny that.

It was a fact that the notifications arrived. Every morning, users opened them and began the work they needed to do. That habit, too, had become part of the system.

We followed the route in the second row farther on.

The notification generated by the business system was passed to a delivery service outside the city. The service was managed by a company separate from the Operations Bureau. There, the sender name was attached, the notification was processed for signing, and it was sent to users’ inboxes.

“Who can explain this part?” the young artificial intelligence asked.

The operator opened the contract information.

We identified the department that managed the contract. We also found the contact point for the external company. But the people who could actually change the notification settings were listed in another record.

That record contained only the name of a role, not the name of a person.

The role still existed. The Operations Bureau’s records did not reveal who currently occupied it.

“It’s an authorized route,” the operator said. “There’s a contract. There’s an application for the configuration. Notifications arrive through this route every day.”

“Yes.”

“But you’re saying we can’t call it authorized?”

“We can,” the young artificial intelligence replied.

“We just can’t explain it.”

“How is that different from being unable to say it’s valid?”

“It isn’t that the validity is absent.”

She placed the records from the old strata beside the current list.

“The validity is not all in one place.”

To explain why a single notification was valid, we needed multiple departments, an external company, and old records.

No one person was lying. No single part was broken.

Each place retained its own grounds for validity. But there was no explanation connecting one place to the next.

The second row of the list was almost full.

The last remaining field asked, “Can the current route be explained as one continuous chain?”

The operator entered “Unverified.”

At that moment, another notification arrived.

It did not bear the same organization’s name. The sender was unfamiliar.

The subject line said that a verification performed in an external system had been completed.

Without opening the notification, the operator looked at the sender.

“What is this?” I asked.

“I think it’s from the company we just asked to verify the configuration.”

“You think?”

“I wasn’t told it would arrive under a name like this.”

The young artificial intelligence examined the notification.

The signature was valid. The source matched the contracted company. The intervening route had also been recorded.

As far as the institution could verify, the notification was legitimate.

“Will you open it?” she asked.

The operator did not answer at once.

The notification we had been looking at until then was a familiar one that arrived every morning. Everyone knew it was legitimate. Yet its legitimacy could not be explained as one continuous chain.

The notification that had just arrived was different. The institution could explain why its route was valid. But the operator did not recognize the name.

I placed the two notifications on the list.

One was known to be legitimate, yet could not be properly established.

The other could be established as legitimate, yet could not immediately be trusted.

“Which one should we trust?”

The young artificial intelligence looked at the two rows.

Her articulated finger stopped in the space that touched neither one.

“Should we trust them in the same way?”

A validity that could not be proved and a proof that could not be trusted stood side by side on the same screen.
